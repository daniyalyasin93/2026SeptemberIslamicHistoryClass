What kind of rebellion?

The sources call all of this ridda — apostasy. Sorted by what people actually did, it separates into at least three different things, and a fourth column of groups who never rebelled at all. Several cases belong in two columns at once, which is where the argument lives.

Cases that resist the columns

Iyas b. Abd Yalil, "al-Fuja'a", of Sulaym asked Abu Bakr for arms to fight apostates and used them to raid Muslims and non-Muslims alike. He was banditry with a cover story, not a rebellion of any type. Abu Bakr had him burned at al-Baqi', and is said to have named it on his deathbed as one of the three acts he regretted.

Groups who had never converted cannot logically have apostatised, yet appear in the campaign narratives all the same — frontier tribes of Quda'a and the north among them. Ask students what work the word ridda is doing when it is applied to them.

On the sources

The classifications here are reconstructions from al-Tabari, al-Baladhuri and Ibn Sa'd, much of it transmitted through Sayf b. Umar, who is widely distrusted and who had reasons to tidy a confused period into a campaign narrative. Later tribal and sectarian interests shaped which rebels were remembered as prophets, which as tax-dodgers, and which were quietly rehabilitated. Where the dispute meter is full, the disagreement is in the sources themselves, not only among modern historians. See Ella Landau-Tasseron on the Ridda traditions, Fred Donner, The Early Islamic Conquests, and Elias Shoufani, Al-Riddah and the Muslim Conquest of Arabia.
